OBJ_0001

Sector Zero

Engine
Unreal 5
Language
C++ / Blueprint
Role
Solo, everything
Status
Proof of concept

A first-person horror game set in a single room. A text file is the physics engine. You play Brian, sent into a dead research site with no memory of arriving. The terminal, RealityTXT, keeps a record of every object in the room, and whatever the record says, reality obeys. Brian has a record too.

This page runs from the goals down to the specifics. The bridge notes on the home page carry the short version; this is the long one.

Credentials come off a note in the room. Tab completion, command history, and a real error on a bad path.

One room, on purpose

The whole game happens in one room. That is not a limit the project ran into, it is the design. A big map spreads a solo budget so thin that every object ends up as a generic interact prompt. One room lets every object get the time it needs, and it makes a standard of polish feasible that a normal-sized game could never afford.

The office chair is the example. It was built until it felt real: it turns to meet you, eases you into the seat, kicks forward in a rhythm, slides, spins, and bounces off walls. Nothing about that was required. But once a chair feels real, a chair is suddenly fun to play with, and that is the bar for everything else in the room.

It is also why every object carries lore. Even things that will never matter mechanically have an ID and a note from whoever handled them before you. A dull object still holds weight for anyone who reads, and the characters get to exist through the things they left behind.

Say nothing

Every object has a three-character ID and a note left by whoever handled it last. Read it, or do not.

Sector Zero is a sandbox. The game explains almost nothing and never waits for you to do the one thing it wanted. You read the notes on objects, you try things, you draw your own conclusions. Most problems have more than one solution, and a player who never finds the intended one has still played the game correctly. This is a game about discovery, not about following.

Do not expect the obvious

Some things make sense. Scissors are sharp, and if you use them as a weapon they behave like one. But this is a place where nothing has to be what it looks like, and the design keeps that freedom on purpose. An object can have a capability that has nothing to do with what it is. A floppy disk could be a teleporter.

That rule teaches the player something valuable early: stop expecting the obvious. From then on the most ordinary object in the room is worth a second look, because it could be anything.

The storm

The room starts broken. On a timer, thunder rolls a strength, and every loose object rolls against it. The ones that lose drop their gravity and drift off with a shove. A few seconds later the screen blinks black and they snap back to where they belong. One of the caught objects comes back changed, marked by whatever the storm is. Stronger thunder means the next one comes sooner.

This is the chaos the game is built around. Things vanish, things move, the room refuses to stay legible.

Register it or lose it

Look at an object, read its ID, type create. The object is now a text file, and the text file is now the object.

The goal is simple: register things so the storm, the chaos and the unknown cannot take them. Point at anything, read its three-character ID, and create writes it into the filesystem as a text file. From then on that file is the object. Edit it and the object follows: rename it, rewrite its note, change what it is. A registered object is stable, readable and usable.

Every save spends from a budget, so attention is the currency. You cannot register everything at once; you choose what matters. Session by session the room gets calmer and starts to make sense, which is the whole arc of the game: chaos first, then an order you built yourself.

Gadgets arrive broken on purpose. The lantern's record reads Condition: Poor, and it plays the part: it flickers, dies, and wants a smack. Half the time you switch it on it holds steady for about ten seconds and then goes back to being itself. A better lantern is one whose file says so.

A real shell

RealityTXT is a working shell over a virtual filesystem, not a menu dressed as one. It runs ls, cd, cat, create, edit, rm, mail, read, reply, logout and reset, with tab completion, command history and real errors. Your login is a line in a file, so editing the file changes your password. help does not list every command. Anyone who has used a terminal already knows how to play, and because every mechanic is a text operation, a new feature costs a command rather than a UI.

The mail client lives inside the shell. Messages arrive, you read them, and replies are numbered options rather than one right answer. It is the cheapest narrative surface in the game and it doubles as the hint system.

Friction, then less of it

Typing is slow, and early on that is the point. Friction makes the room feel like a workplace and the game feel down to earth. But a terminal mechanic can turn tedious fast, so the design does not lean on it. The terminal is kept for the moments that need it, balanced against actually exploring the room, and later in the game tools automate registering so the friction fades as you get better. Early friction is realism. Late friction would just be waste.

The chair

Kick, slide, spin, bounce. Tank controls on an office chair.

Sit and the chair turns to face you, waits half a second, then eases you into the seat with the camera. Forward is not a speed, it is a kick in three beats: a weak push, a strong push, a weak push, then a pause. The chair keeps almost all of its speed from frame to frame, so it slides, and it brakes a little harder right after a kick so it never feels like ice. Turning builds up and winds down with its own inertia, loose when fast and grippy when slow. Hit a wall and it reflects with a small boost and ignores input for a quarter of a second, so a bounce feels like a bounce.

You can work the whole room without standing up. That is the standard the single room makes affordable: in one room a chair is a tenth of the furniture, so it gets built like it matters.

Brian is an object

Weight, height, sex, age, password. Save the file and Brian changes.

Brian is a middle-aged man who is heavy and out of shape. He walks slowly. Crouching is slow and uncomfortable. That is not a movement setting, it is his worker record: a text file with his weight, height, sex, age and login. Weight drives walk speed, jump and gravity. Height rescales the whole character and keeps his feet on the floor. Go past two and a half metres and the file kills him.

Mass, not fat

Find the medical records and you can rewrite him. Type a lower weight and he moves like a healthy man, and he can suddenly fit through gaps that were closed to him before. Type a negative weight and gravity flips: he falls onto the ceiling, the controls invert with him, and he gets thrown out of the chair. The number is his mass, not his fat percentage. What the file says, he is. It is the same rule as every other object in the room, applied to the player.

The dark

The horror works because you never leave. You learn where everything sits, so even a minor change reads loudly, in a way it never could in a game that keeps moving you somewhere new. A mug that moved is worse than a jump scare.

Something shares the room. It is dark, hard to see, and never fully shown. Maybe it is one of the other three. Maybe it is something else. The game does not say, and the player is left to draw their own conclusions. It throws objects. It reaches the terminal and deletes progress or corrupts files. No music announces it, so you never know whether you are safe. Hit it with a thrown weapon while it hides and it goes still for a while, the only breathing room you get.

It should not stay that hard. Early on the creature is the big problem. Over time the player gets better tools, and later traps, that push it back and maybe solve it for good, instead of being stuck repeating the same defence forever. Brian starts as a victim and ends as the master of the room. None of this is in the build yet; it is the plan the room is being built to hold.

Obscura Dynamics

Obscura Dynamics researches anomalies. Secretive, very well funded, and decades into trying to decode why reality misbehaves, they noticed that certain anomalies shared a component that was not matter, sound or light. They named it Anomium. Anything that touches it suffers pure chaos: combustion, transmutation, erasure. Long exposure breaks minds and bodies.

They built devices to detect it, then embedded it safely in a reinforced computer, and after a run of catastrophic failures produced RealityTXT: a terminal that gives control over any physical object listed inside it. Type static on an object and it holds, even against Anomium. For the first time the company had a weapon against chaos. What Anomium actually is, they still do not know.

Four people in debt

A remote Obscura facility called Sector Zero went silent after an experiment got away from them, with vital RealityTXT data still inside. Rather than risk staff, Obscura recruited people in serious financial trouble, told them the risk plainly, and had them sign. It was human experimentation with a contract.

Brian was a schoolteacher until anxiety and depression cost him the job. He is gentle, easily swayed, and has been with Amy for years. Amy ran corporate operations until a corruption accusation ended her career; she leads by force of will and gets more controlling under pressure. Laura was a nurse until one traumatic night; she panics, and she still puts everyone else first. Jason was a mechanic with gambling debts; he is careless, and his carelessness occasionally finds solutions nobody else would.

The game opens after the four have been separated. You are Brian, alone, without memories, piecing together what happened to you and to them. The creature is the threat. The corporation that sent you is the antagonist.

Where it stands

Built: the shell and its filesystem, login off a file, the register and edit loop with its budget, the worker record with weight, height and inverted gravity, the storm cycle with its blackout snap, the chair, a screwdriver that charges for three seconds and flies back to where you threw it from, and a lantern that lies about working.

Next: the creature, the traps, the tools that automate registering, the medical records. Sector Zero is a proof of concept on purpose. Prove one room can carry a game, then fill it.
