# Phase 8 research: the tailored one

## Gilded Age cut (1870-1900)
- victorianweb.org/art/costume/nunn24.html (Nunn, Victorian outerwear): Ulster runs almost to the ankle, is caped and belted, and is heavy travel wear. Gladstone is shorter and double-breasted with an astrakhan edge. Evening coats had velvet collars. So a long hem plus a fur edge was real period dress, not fantasy.
- gentlemansgazette.com/chesterfield-overcoat: Chesterfield is the city coat, knee length, no cape, velvet collar, a clean straight line. Take its restraint and give it the Ulster's length: one unbroken column, no cape, since a cape widens the shoulder mass.
- fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/frock-coat: the frock coat's skirt runs all round to the knee and the waist is seamed and fitted. This is the source of "fit reads as warmth": the waist seam gives the pinch, and the skirt flares below it.
- permanentstyle.com, capricebespoke.com (Savile Row fit): strong, fairly square shoulder; close chest; suppressed waist; a slightly long jacket that flares a little at the hem; a high sleeve head. Trousers hang straight and just touch the shoe (no pooling). In silhouette: a V from shoulder to waist, a small flare, then straight vertical legs.
- en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top_hat, silktophats.eu: the stovepipe crown was about 8 in (20 cm), with theatre extremes near 12 in. Late-century crowns were 16-17 cm and slightly nipped at the waist. Our hat pushes past history on purpose, and the nipped crown is a period-true detail that also stops it reading as a plain rectangle.

## Proportion as class and menace
- gameinformer.com (Dishonored 2 art direction), pcgamer.com (Mitton concept notes): aristocrats are slender and tall, clothes are sharply tailored and bodies are caricatured. Principle: exaggerate the body, keep the tailoring crisp. Class is the clean cut edge. Menace is the stretched length (long shins, long forearms, hands a little too big). Our one rule to borrow: exaggerate proportion and keep the garment geometry believable.
- Fashion croquis, 9 to 12 heads (vizcom.com/blog/fashion-croquis, dishafashioninstitute.com): fashion drawings add their extra height almost entirely to the legs and a little to the torso, never to the shoulders. The same trick makes elegance: length goes below the waist.

## Headless figures
- en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Son_of_Man, artsy.net (Magritte's bowler hats): the hat stands for identity. A hat over a hidden face reads as "a respectable man" with nothing under it. The hat has to sit level and correct, because the joke only works if the dress is perfectly proper.
- propstore.com (Sleepy Hollow 1999 Horseman, Colleen Atwood): a tall stiff collar carries the neck line upward so the missing head reads as absent rather than cropped. The cape and collar frame an empty space.
- Slender Man (Knudsen, 2009, per creepypasta.com and supernatural-saga.com summaries): plain dark suit, no face, limbs a little too long. Proportion alone, with ordinary dress, is enough for dread. No extra appendages needed.

## Past the list
- Edward Gorey (goreyana.blogspot.com, lithub.com): his recurring figure is a man sunk in a huge fur coat, hat on top, sneakers poking out below. The fur is a smooth mass with a scalloped edge, not strands. Gorey goes for bulk; we keep only his fur treatment (a solid mass with a scalloped outline) and limit it to the collar and cuffs.
- Aubrey Beardsley (tate.org.uk, en.wikipedia.org): elongated figures in large flat black masses, with meaning carried by the outline and a few white slivers. This is direct permission for a flat single-colour fill: the edges do all the work.
- Tim Burton / Atwood generally: height comes from verticals (hat, collar, cane, pinstripe) stacked on one axis.

## Design takeaways (units = fraction of total height H, hat top to boot sole)
1. Hat 25-32% of H. Crown sides taper inward 5-8% toward the top (nipped). Brim width about 1.4x the crown base, brim thickness under 1.5% of H.
2. Gap between collar top and hat brim: 2-4% of H of empty background. The hat floats level over the void; there is no neck. Hat tilt at most 5 degrees.
3. Fur collar stands 4-6% of H above the shoulder line and is 1.3-1.5x shoulder width only at the collar. Scalloped outline with 5-7 lobes per side, no strands.
4. Shoulder width 22-26% of H, square, and the widest point of the figure apart from the collar and the brim.
5. Waist at 42-46% of H from the top, suppressed to 55-65% of shoulder width. Waist is the narrowest point of the coat.
6. Coat hem at mid-calf, 78-84% of H from the top. Skirt flares to about 80% of shoulder width; no cape.
7. Legs (crotch to sole) at least 45% of the body below the brim; the body below the waist is at least 55% of H. Shins show below the hem as two straight thin columns, each about 5% of H wide.
8. Trousers break once on the boot. Boots are narrow, with a pointed toe 1.2-1.5x leg width and a small heel. The feet are the only thing that points forward.
9. Hands slightly large (glove length about 7% of H). Cane from glove to ground, thin (under 1% of H), set about 10% of H outside the hem line so it reads as a separate stroke.
10. Readability test: at 0.05 of screen height, the flat fill must still show four notches: the brim/gap, the collar bump, the waist pinch, and the hem/leg split. Anything smaller than 1.5% of H gets dropped from the silhouette.
